I The Vision

Inhabit exists to carry coherent Christian identity, ethic, and public posture into the next century and a half: to build ecclesial infrastructure rooted enough, embodied enough, and durable enough to outlast whatever is presently coming apart.

We are not a renewal movement. We are not a program of impact aimed at existing church structures. We are a society of common life, building parallel infrastructure designed to be inhabited rather than attended.

The formal name carries the vision. Mansio: a dwelling, a way-station, a semi-permanent settlement along the road. And the Society for Gospel Living, not gospel impact or gospel renewal. Living is what the Society is; impact and renewal are what God does through faithful presence over generations. We build for long obedience in the same direction1 and the patient formation, for the cathedral whose completion we will not see.

II A Gospel Thin Enough to Die Of

The deepest trouble in the Western church is not in the culture, where we would much prefer to find it, but where we least want to look—in what we have come to believe the gospel is, and in what we have allowed the church to become.

We have suffered a long identity crisis over what the gospel even is: eternal fire insurance against a future we would rather not consider, or the present power of Christ to convert nations, remake cultures, and overturn individual human lives. We chose the insurance. It costs less.

The result is a church haunted by the gospel rather than characterized by it. The safeguards God wove into the way of Jesus we have explained away with our theological sophistications; the signposts meant to mark the faithful from the forlorn have been blunted until there is little tension left between believer and skeptic. We are doctors whose patients are screaming for the cure we have set aside, even as we quietly take up our patients’ favorite addictions.

The dominant church-planting models have accelerated rather than resisted this. The seeker-sensitive, leader-centric, funding-hungry paradigm produces congregations that function as spiritual supplements to secular life rather than comprehensive alternatives. Christians gather on Sunday and live as functional pagans the rest of the week: embedded in secular liturgies, their vocations un-transformed, their economic lives indistinguishable from their neighbors’, their witness diluted into political activism or therapeutic accommodation. Attendance and budgets are measured; formation is not. Congregations grow by transfer rather than conversion. Members consume religious goods and exit when preferences are not met.

This is not a management failure. It is idolatry. Consumer spirituality is not a deficient form of Christianity; it is, by the most careful definitions of the tradition, the love of self wearing the language of the love of God. The immortal God whose demands are total has been traded for a constructed deity who reflects the consumer’s values, affirms her preferences, and asks nothing she was not already inclined to give.

Whatever else the coming century holds (and there is reason to think it holds less stability than the last), a gospel this thin will not survive it. The hour demands more than replication. It demands reformation.

III The Thick Gospel

When Paul wants to define “the word of faith that we proclaim,” he does a startling thing. He quotes Deuteronomy. The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart. So speaks Moses, in Deuteronomy 30, preaching to Israel on the boundary of the land. Paul does not treat the First Testament as the gospel’s runway, a long stretch of law before grace finally lifts off. He treats a sermon of Moses as the gospel he himself preaches.

Deuteronomy is not primarily a legal code but the great declaration of God’s covenant love, in which obedience is never the price of redemption but grateful response to it. Grace runs ahead of law, but never outstrips it. Israel is rescued from Egypt first, and only then taught what it means to be human again. And the order in which Moses teaches them is belong, believe, behave. They belong before they believe: redeemed, named, brought near: a people before they are catechized. They believe: Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one. And they behave: the love of God woven into doorposts and textiles, beaten into walking paths, and the hum drum of rising up and lying down, and, at its summit, the love of the seeker: you shall love the stranger, for you were strangers in Egypt.

Salvation, it turns out, is an invitation to imitation (we extend the welcome we ourselves received), and in our stumbling after our Redeemer, we build homes worth inhabiting. Because, to our surprise, evangelism is the practice of hospitality. The seeker, the ger, the sojourner who casts his lot with Israel, who keeps her Sabbath, who receives her covenant sign, sits at her Passover table. Israel loves the stranger by drawing him toward what she is, not by dissolving into what he was. The open door beckons. A pattern of vibrant living awaits.

Belong, believe, behave is not a ladder to be climbed once. It is a flywheel. Belonging grounds believing. Believing grounds behaving. And behaving, when it matures, turns outward as hospitality and creates new belonging. The wheel gaining mass with every revolution, until the community’s ordinary life is itself the engine and the display of its mission. Each element fails without the others. Belonging without belief or behavior becomes tribal. Belief without belonging or behavior becomes fundamentalist. Behavior without belonging or belief becomes legalist. Turning together, they become a people. A witness. An invitation.

This is the thick gospel: a God who creates, who binds himself in covenant, who redeems before he commands, who forms a distinct people in order that they might be salt and light, and who means, in Christ, to expand from Israel after the flesh into the Israel of God, to use St. Paul’s distinction. The Gospel does not consist of propositions to be filed and forgotten. It is the truth, it offers life, and it is a way of living as a people who belong to a God, and to one another. Glorious in its complexity, effective in its design, and drawing irresistible attention to its Designer, the Gospel is, indeed, good news—not just for tomorrow, but also for today.

IV The Wall and the Open Gate

If the church must become a distinct people, formed apart from a culture that deforms it, do we not simply withdraw? Is this not the cloister, the gated enclave, the holy huddle with the drawbridge up?

It is not, and while Dreher reached for the Benedict Option, I shall be tutored by Nehemiah.

When Nehemiah arrives in the land, the Jews have been trickling back from Babylon for the better part of a century, and they are home and yet not home. Back in the land but overrun, their identity dissolved into the peoples around them, indistinguishable and therefore powerless. Nehemiah’s first work is a wall. And a wall sounds, to modern ears, exactly like withdrawal, or rejection. But the moment they have it, the Israelites fling the gates open and keep them open six days in seven; they read the Book of the Law aloud; the largest revival in the Old Testament breaks out; and a people who could not be told apart from their neighbors becomes a people worth noticing.

The wall is not the opposite of hospitality. It is its precondition. A gate is an opening in a wall—an entry, on terms—not the absence of one. A community with no wall has no gate to open, because it has nothing to invite anyone into; it has been overrun by that which surrounds it. They will know us by our love, Jesus said, but they must first see an us to even notice. According to Deuteronomy 4:6–8, it is the distinctiveness of God’s people that will attract the watching seekers.

The Nehemiah option leaves us a sort of blueprint. First the wall enables a recoverable, sustainable common identity. Then the people are settled and ordered for worship. Then the Book of Instruction is read and explained so they understand it. The understanding produces confession, and the confession rises into worship, and the worship gives way to covenant: a people binding themselves to a Sovereign and to His way of life. So, the covenant orders the conduct of the city: its calendar, its commerce, its care. That is not a program. It is a house of formation, drawn straight out of our constituting text. Or, as St. Paul put it, it’s what transforms us by the renewing of our very selves.

V The Shape of the Thing

The structural archetype is the Celtic abbey, not the enclosed Benedictine monastery. It is a generative center of gravity that houses the fully committed and radiates outward into lay settlements, farms, schools, and craft communities that share its life without being absorbed by it. Its power lies in its life, not its decrees. This is paired with the minster tradition—the mother church of a region from which ministry radiates to surrounding congregations. The two correct each other: the minster keeps the abbey outward-facing, while the abbey keeps the minster rooted in disciplined common life rather than hollow institutional maintenance.

The abbey is the formational core, where the deeply committed embody a rule of life. Its authority is generative rather than jurisdictional; it forms the people who will inhabit, lead, and plant elsewhere. An abbey-village ensures the minster at its center can never be divorced from worship as a way of life. The minster, by contrast, is pastoral and congregation-facing. It keeps formation oriented toward mission rather than an end in itself. It is a relational hub whose reach cannot exceed the bonds of relationship; the network and the minster sustain one another.

Surrounding communities are congregations that share the same confession and common life—not mere Sunday gatherings but full social ecosystems of schools, work, craft, agriculture, and mutual aid. Their shared life is itself the primary public witness. They look to the minster as a resource center, knowing it is their life that sustains it.

Inhabit is a mission society. What it offers is not an institution but a way: a way of reading Scripture as one covenant word and a way of inhabiting the gospel together that can hold a people across generations. Institutions are downstream of that. Better to resource a hundred congregations well than to govern ten. The hoped-for fruit is consociation—congregations knitting themselves together in shared goods, labor, teaching, and obligation until the common life becomes answerable for itself across a district, then a nation, then a region. Whether that body ever takes formal canonical shape is not the measure of the work. The Society serves congregations across the Western hemisphere without seeking to extract them from the bodies that already hold them. Where a congregation lacks a home, we plan to be able to answer.

The first settlement is in the Panamanian highlands outside Volcán, Chiriquí, with Corazón del Cielo as the pilot congregation. Land, existing houses, planned chapel and parsonage, a nine-year Bible study, and terrain suited to village life make it practical. Deeper still is the need for distance from the unexamined gravitational pull of American cultural assumptions—about efficiency, individualism, the nature of a church service, and the meaning of success. Panama supplies enough remove to force the distinction between Christian habit and American inheritance, yet remains near enough that North Americans can function. As the isthmus of the continents and home of the Canal, Panama is uniquely suited for a proof of concept.

VI The Settlement Is the Sermon

When Jesus sent the eleven out, we have learned to hear one word above the others: Go. As though the whole weight of the commission fell on motion, on the campaign, on getting somewhere fast. But the one imperative in the sentence, the main verb around which everything else turns, is not “go.” It is make disciples. The going, the baptizing, the teaching all hang upon that single command and serve it.

We are heirs of a revivalism that made the gospel into an event and the Christian life into a perpetual altar call, all urgency, motion and decision, and it has left us anxious, exhausted and strangely unformed. A disciple is an apprentice: a learner who takes a master’s understanding of life and works it into every corner of his own. One does not manufacture apprentices in a weekend. You form them slowly, in a place, among a people, over years. To recover the main verb is to recover patience.

But the patient making of disciples does not cancel the sending; it grounds it. Make disciples of all nations still faces outward. It still means the stranger and the foreigner and the neighbor who does not yet belong. The only question is how. And the answer the whole of Scripture keeps giving is: by building a life so visibly good that the nations can be invited into it as sojourners, invited to come and see what human life looks like when it is ordered toward God, and to stay, and to be formed, and over time to find that they too belong, and believe, and behave. You cannot teach the nations to observe all that Christ commanded unless there is somewhere they can come and watch it being observed. The settlement, you understand, is the sermon.

VII The Long Horizon, Honestly

We are not building for ourselves. We are building for generations we will not see, planting forests whose shade we will not enjoy, establishing communities whose maturity we will not witness. The medieval cathedral builders began projects they knew would outlast them. That is the disposition the covenant requires, and it is the disposition Inhabit means to cultivate.

Communities like this also fail, and they fail in known ways: founder dependency; boundaries that harden from membrane into wall; a second generation that inherits the forms without the fire; isolation mistaken for holiness; and the particular danger of authority that is formational rather than jurisdictional, the kind that shapes lives deeply while being reviewable by no one. We do not consider ourselves exempt from any of it. Our polity, our confession, and our submission to episcopal oversight exist precisely as guards against our own most likely failures, and we would rather name them at the outset than be surprised by them in twenty years.

VIII Let Us Plant Accordingly

Long ago, to a people who had built dams across the gospel’s own river and then wondered why the water no longer ran, the Lord said: stand at the crossroads and look; ask for the ancient paths, ask where the good way is, and walk in it, and you will find rest for your souls. The way is not new. It was never new. It is the oldest way there is: a people belonging to God, believing what he has spoken, and behaving as those who are loved, with their gates open to the stranger just as they were once the welcomed strangers.

Rest for your souls. It sounds like a distant country. It is, in fact, only the ancient path; it is still open, and it still leads home.

We are asking for three things: people willing to come and be formed and to form others; counsel from those who have built things meant to last; and the resources to make a first settlement viable long enough to prove what it claims.
